# UK Online Safety Act 2023 — Obligations for a Child-Accessible Learning Platform

**Researched:** 2026-11-19  
**Platform profile:** UK-based, accessible to under-18s (school students); adults (teachers) publish educational course content that minors subscribe to and consume; no direct messaging between users; not school-scoped (any learner can subscribe to any teacher).

---

## 1. Age Assurance: Is it Required, and What Mechanism Does Ofcom Specify?

### Does the platform fall in scope?

The platform is a **user-to-user (U2U) service** as defined in section 3 of the Online Safety Act 2023. A U2U service is any internet service by means of which content generated or uploaded by a user may be encountered by another user — it does not matter what proportion of content is user-generated, nor whether sharing actually occurs, only that the functionality exists. Teachers publishing course content that students can access satisfies this definition.

**Schedule 1 educational exemption does not apply.** The OSA Schedule 1, paragraph 10 exempts services provided by entities with legal responsibility for specified education/childcare, or by those employed with safeguarding duties _for that specific education_. An independent, open-access platform (any learner can subscribe to any teacher) is not provided under the responsibility of a school or registered institution, so the exemption is unavailable.

### Children's access assessment (mandatory for all U2U services)

All in-scope U2U and search services must complete a **children's access assessment** to determine whether their service is likely to be accessed by children. The deadline was 16 April 2025. For a platform that markets itself to school students, this assessment will almost certainly conclude that children are likely to access the service, triggering children's safety duties.

### Age assurance obligation

Where a service is determined to be likely accessed by children and hosts content in Ofcom's risk tiers, the **Protection of Children Codes of Practice** (published 24 April 2025, in force from 25 July 2025) require **highly effective age assurance (HEAA)** in the following circumstances:

- The service disseminates **primary priority content** (pornography, suicide/self-harm, eating disorder promotion) or **priority content** (abuse, bullying, violent content) **and** is assessed as medium-to-high risk.
- The service's recommender system handles medium-to-high risk material.
- The service prohibits such content in its terms but cannot technically enforce removal.

For an educational platform where teacher content is educational course material with no primary priority or priority harmful content, **mandatory HEAA is unlikely to be triggered automatically.** However:

- The platform must still complete a children's risk assessment and implement appropriate risk-proportionate protective measures.
- If harmful content could reach students (e.g., via uploaded course materials), HEAA would be required.
- Ofcom advises services to "err on the side of caution."

**Acceptable HEAA mechanisms specified by Ofcom** (from January 2025 guidance on highly effective age assurance):
- Photo-ID matching
- Facial age estimation
- Mobile network operator (MNO) age checks
- Email-based age estimation
- Digital identity services

**Unacceptable methods:** self-declaration, payment card checks not requiring age verification, terms-of-service restrictions alone.

## 2. Content Moderation Obligations for Teacher-Published Content

### Illegal content duties (in force from 17 March 2025)

All regulated U2U services, regardless of size, must:

1. **Complete an illegal content risk assessment** (ongoing, must be updated).
2. **Implement proportionate measures** to prevent users from encountering priority illegal content (terrorism, CSAM, intimate image abuse, public order offences, assisting suicide, etc.).
3. **Swift takedown** of any illegal content once alerted or discovered — systems must minimise the time such content is present.
4. **Maintain written records** of risk assessments.

### Children's safety duties (codes in force from 25 July 2025)

For services likely accessed by children, the **Protection of Children Code of Practice** requires:

- **Children's risk assessment** — assess harm risk to different age groups from content on the platform, including from teacher-uploaded course materials.
- **Risk-proportionate protective measures** including:
  - Content moderation systems able to identify primary priority content (pornography, suicide/self-harm promotion, eating disorder content), priority content (abuse, bullying, violent content, dangerous challenges), and non-designated content (NDC) presenting material risk of significant harm.
  - **Prevent children of any age** from encountering primary priority content.
  - **Protect at-risk age groups** from priority content.
  - Report mechanisms — users (and others) must be able to report harmful content; a complaints procedure is required.
  - Recommender system configuration to filter harmful content from children's feeds where operated.
- **Named senior accountable person** for children's safety compliance.
- **Annual senior-body review** of children's safety risk management.

### Practical implication for teacher content

Teacher-published educational course content is not, itself, in a harmful category. However:

- The platform must have **moderation capability** to review uploaded content for illegal material (CSAM is an absolute floor regardless of platform type).
- It must have a user-facing **reporting mechanism** for harmful content and a **complaints procedure**.
- If a teacher uploaded content that fell into a harmful category (e.g., pro-eating-disorder material), the platform would be legally required to act swiftly to remove it.
- A children's risk assessment covering the content moderation approach is mandatory.

## 3. Teacher/Creator Identity Obligations

### What the OSA says

The Online Safety Act 2023 **does not impose a specific identity verification or DBS-equivalent obligation on platforms at the platform level** for content creators or publishers. The Act's focus is on content safety and risk, not on verifying the credentials or background of users who publish content.

### What the OSA does require (indirectly relevant)

- **Terms of service enforcement:** Platforms must enforce their published community standards and policies. If a platform's terms restrict publishing to verified educators, those terms must be enforced consistently.
- **Risk assessment scope:** A children's risk assessment under s.11 must assess the risk that the platform could be misused — including risks arising from adult users having access to minors. If the platform permits direct or indirect contact between teachers and students (even one-way content subscription), this is a factor in the risk assessment.
- **Safety by design:** Platforms must consider design features that could create risks for children, including functionalities that allow adult users to reach minors.

### KCSIE 2024 context (not OSA — but relevant to spec)

Keeping Children Safe in Education (KCSIE) 2024 governs schools' responsibilities for DBS-checking staff and volunteers. KCSIE does **not** impose obligations on independent online platforms — it applies to schools and educational settings. If the platform were used in a school context, the school would bear the KCSIE obligation to DBS-check teachers; the platform itself has no KCSIE duty.

However, the **reputational and safeguarding risk** is significant: if any adult accesses minors through the platform, the platform bears OSA risk assessment liability, potential ICO enforcement, and public trust exposure. Best practice (and arguably necessary for a reasonable children's risk assessment) would be to implement **teacher identity verification** as a platform-level safeguard, even though it is not explicitly mandated by law.

### No sector-specific "platform-level DBS" requirement exists

There is currently no statutory requirement for general online learning platforms to run DBS checks on content creators. This is a gap that the concept spec should acknowledge — the platform should consider a voluntary safeguarding framework (e.g., a code of conduct, identity verification, and self-declaration of DBS status) as a trust signal.

## 4. Ofcom Registration or Notification Requirement

### Fee-based notification (s.83 OSA)

Providers of regulated services with a **qualifying worldwide revenue (QWR) of £250 million or more** must notify Ofcom and pay online safety fees under the Online Safety Act 2023 (Fees) (Threshold Figure) Regulations 2025 (SI 2025/1204).

An additional **UK-referable revenue exemption** applies: providers whose UK revenue is less than £10 million in the qualifying period are exempt even if their worldwide revenue meets the threshold.

**A startup learning platform will fall below the £250 million QWR threshold.** The fee and notification obligation does not apply.

### Operational compliance (not formal registration)

There is **no separate universal registration requirement** for all regulated services. The OSA does not require all in-scope services to register with Ofcom before operating. Compliance obligations are self-executing — the platform must:

1. Assess whether the Act applies (it does).
2. Conduct and document illegal content risk assessments.
3. Conduct and document children's access assessments.
4. Conduct and document children's risk assessments (if children are likely to access the platform).
5. Implement the required safety measures.
6. Maintain records (which Ofcom can request at any time).

Ofcom has an **enforcement programme** to monitor whether services are meeting their children's risk assessment duties, and can initiate investigations. The OSA creates penalties of up to £18 million or 10% of global revenue (whichever is higher) for non-compliance.

## 5. ICO Children's Code and UK GDPR Obligations for Child Users

### Scope: the Children's Code applies

The ICO's Age Appropriate Design Code (Children's Code), which is statutory under s.123 of the Data Protection Act 2018, applies to any **information society service** likely to be accessed by children under 18 — regardless of whether the service targets children. An open-access UK learning platform marketed at school students is squarely in scope.

### The 15 Standards

The Code's 15 standards require:

1. **Best interests of the child** — processing decisions must prioritise child welfare.
2. **Data protection impact assessments (DPIAs)** — required before launching or making significant changes.
3. **Age-appropriate application** — safeguards must be calibrated by risk and by the ages of users accessing the service.
4. **Transparency** — privacy notices in clear, age-appropriate language.
5. **Detrimental use of data** — no processing that works against children's wellbeing.
6. **Policies and community standards** — enforce published data protection policies consistently.
7. **Default settings** — high privacy by default; public profiles off by default.
8. **Data minimisation** — collect only the minimum personal data necessary for the service.
9. **Data sharing** — no disclosure to third parties without compelling justification aligned with the child's interests.
10. **Geolocation** — off by default unless compelling reason exists.
11. **Parental controls** — provide transparent monitoring options in age-appropriate language.
12. **Profiling** — algorithmic profiling off by default; no behavioural advertising to children.
13. **Nudge techniques** — no manipulative design patterns that weaken privacy choices.
14. **Connected toys/devices** — IoT protections (less relevant here).
15. **Online tools** — accessible mechanisms for children to exercise data rights (access, deletion, opt-out).

### UK GDPR age of consent (s.9 DPA 2018)

Under the UK GDPR (applied via s.9 Data Protection Act 2018), the age at which a child can provide their own consent for processing their personal data in the context of an information society service is **13 years old**.

- **Under 13:** Parental/guardian consent is required (unless a counselling/preventive service exemption applies). The platform must make **reasonable efforts** to verify parental responsibility.
- **13 and over:** The child may consent independently, but consent must still be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous.

Consent is not the only lawful basis — legitimate interests or performance of a contract may apply — but each must be assessed carefully for child-data contexts.

### Key practical obligations beyond standard GDPR

| Obligation | Standard GDPR | Children's Code / Child-Specific |
|---|---|---|
| Privacy by default | Required | _High_ privacy by default |
| Data minimisation | Required | Strictly minimum; separate choices per feature |
| Transparency | Clear, understandable | Age-appropriate language (not legalistic) |
| Profiling | Requires lawful basis | Off by default; no behavioural advertising |
| Consent age | (EU: 16, member state can lower) | UK: 13 |
| Geolocation | Requires lawful basis | Off by default |
| DPIAs | Required for high risk | Required _before_ launch for child-accessible services |
| Nudge techniques | Not addressed | Prohibited |

### Enforcement

The ICO enforces the Children's Code. Fines can reach £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover (whichever is higher). The ICO has used enforcement notices and published investigations against major platforms (e.g., TikTok, Snap) for Children's Code breaches.

| Area | Key Obligation | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| OSA in-scope status | Platform is a U2U service; Schedule 1 educational exemption unavailable | Immediate |
| Children's access assessment | Must assess whether children are likely to access; answer is yes | Was due April 2025 (ongoing) |
| Illegal content risk assessment | Mandatory; written record required | In force March 2025 |
| Children's risk assessment | Mandatory once children's access confirmed; written record required | Was due July 2025 (ongoing) |
| Content moderation | Illegal content removal; reporting mechanism; complaints process | In force |
| Children's safety duties | Risk-proportionate measures; prevent primary priority content; protect at-risk age groups | In force July 2025 |
| Age assurance (HEAA) | Required if medium-high risk of harmful content; not automatically required for a low-risk educational platform | Triggered by risk assessment |
| OSA notification/fees | Not required below £250M QWR | N/A for startup |
| ICO Children's Code | Privacy by default; data minimisation; no profiling/nudging; age-appropriate transparency; DPIA before launch | Existing law |
| UK GDPR age of consent | Under-13s need parental consent for data processing; 13+ can self-consent | Existing law |
| Teacher identity | No statutory DBS-equivalent at platform level; but risk assessment likely requires mitigation measures | Best practice / indirect obligation |
